# Conceptual Metaphor Theory and the Embodiment Thesis

## Introduction

Since its inception in 1980 with Lakoff and Johnson's "Metaphors We Live By," Conceptual Metaphor Theory (CMT) has fundamentally challenged traditional views of metaphor as merely ornamental language. CMT positions metaphor as a central cognitive mechanism through which humans understand abstract concepts by mapping them onto more concrete, experientially grounded domains. The theory's revolutionary claim is that metaphor is not primarily linguistic but conceptual, structuring how we think, reason, and act. This cognitive approach is deeply intertwined with the embodiment thesis, which asserts that human cognition is fundamentally shaped by bodily experience and sensorimotor interactions with the world. This essay outlines the main claims of CMT and critically examines its relationship to the embodiment thesis, arguing that while embodiment provides crucial grounding for primary metaphors, the relationship becomes more complex with higher-order conceptual structures and cultural variation.

## The Core Claims of Conceptual Metaphor Theory

Conceptual Metaphor Theory rests on several fundamental claims that distinguish it from classical theories of metaphor. First and most centrally, CMT argues that **metaphor is primarily conceptual rather than linguistic**. When we say "That argument is indefensible" or "He attacked every weak point in my argument," we are not simply using colorful language; rather, we are revealing an underlying conceptual metaphor ARGUMENT IS WAR that structures how we understand, conduct, and discuss argumentation. The linguistic expressions are merely surface manifestations of deeper conceptual mappings. This represents a significant departure from traditional rhetoric, which treated metaphor as a matter of words rather than thought.

Second, CMT claims that **conceptual metaphors are systematic and productive**. The ARGUMENT IS WAR metaphor generates a coherent system: we "win" or "lose" arguments, "defend" positions, "attack" claims, or find arguments "bulletproof." This systematicity suggests that metaphorical understanding follows consistent mappings between domains. Moreover, these metaphors are productive, generating novel expressions that remain comprehensible because speakers share the underlying conceptual structure.

Third, CMT posits that **metaphorical mappings are unidirectional**, flowing from a concrete source domain to a more abstract target domain. We understand arguments in terms of war, but we do not understand war in terms of arguments. This unidirectionality is not accidental; it reflects the experiential basis of metaphorical thought. We can reason about the abstract concept of argument by drawing on our more concrete, embodied understanding of physical conflict and defense. Critically, this claim has implications for what can serve as a source domain—typically, it must be something more directly grounded in physical or social experience.

Fourth, CMT argues that **metaphor is ubiquitous in ordinary thought and language**, not restricted to poetry or rhetoric. Our everyday conceptual system is fundamentally metaphorical. Consider how we talk about time: "The deadline is approaching," "The worst is behind us," "We're coming up on Christmas." These expressions reveal the conceptual metaphor TIME IS SPACE, specifically treating time as a landscape through which we move or which moves past us. We rarely recognize these as metaphors because they are so deeply embedded in our conceptual apparatus. This ubiquity suggests that metaphorical thought is not a special cognitive capacity deployed only in creative contexts but a basic mechanism of human understanding.

A critical fifth claim concerns **inference and entailment patterns**. Conceptual metaphors are not merely about mapping vocabulary from one domain to another; they preserve inferential structure. The source domain comes with its own internal logic, and when we map it onto a target domain, we import that logic as well. For instance, the metaphor LOVE IS A JOURNEY entails that lovers are travelers, the relationship is a vehicle, difficulties are obstacles on the path, and relationship goals correspond to destinations. Crucially, if a journey can be derailed, stuck, or reach a dead end, so too can a relationship in our metaphorical understanding. This means metaphors don't just label abstract concepts; they shape how we reason about them.

## Domains, Mappings, and Entailments: The Mechanics of CMT

To understand how conceptual metaphors work, we must examine their internal structure. A conceptual metaphor involves systematic correspondences between a **source domain** (the concrete, well-understood domain) and a **target domain** (the abstract domain being understood). These correspondences are called **mappings**. In the metaphor ANGER IS HEATED FLUID IN A CONTAINER, the source domain is our physical understanding of heated fluids and pressurized containers, while the target domain is the emotional experience of anger.

The mappings specify element-by-element correspondences: the container maps onto the body, the heat maps onto intensity of anger, the pressure maps onto internal pressure when angry, and explosion maps onto losing control. These preserve what Lakoff and Johnson call the "invariance principle"—the image-schematic structure of the source domain constrains how it maps onto the target.

What makes this powerful is that mappings preserve **entailment structures**. As heated fluid in a closed container builds pressure and eventually explodes, so too we understand anger: as anger intensifies, internal pressure increases ("I was about to explode"); without controlled release ("letting off steam"), it manifests uncontrollably ("He just blew up at me"). The metaphor provides a coherent framework for reasoning about anger's causes, experience, and consequences.

Grady's work on **primary metaphors** adds crucial nuance. Primary metaphors are basic mappings arising from universal correlations in embodied experience: AFFECTION IS WARMTH (from being held warmly by caregivers), IMPORTANCE IS SIZE (bigger people are more powerful), and DIFFICULTY IS HEAVINESS (heavier objects require more effort).

These primary metaphors combine to form **complex metaphors**. THEORIES ARE BUILDINGS, for instance, combines ABSTRACT STRUCTURE IS PHYSICAL STRUCTURE with other primary metaphors, explaining why theories have "foundations," can "collapse," or need "support." The compound nature accounts for both systematicity (inheriting coherence from primary metaphors) and cultural variation (different cultures combine them differently).

Critically, not all source domain aspects map onto targets. These **unused mappings** reveal that metaphorical understanding is selective. ARGUMENT IS WAR highlights adversarial aspects but obscures collaborative dimensions; we don't extend it to "prisoners of war" or "post-war reconstruction," suggesting mappings preserve relevance to actual experience.

## Embodiment and the Grounding of Conceptual Metaphor

The relationship between Conceptual Metaphor Theory and the embodiment thesis is both foundational and complex. The embodiment thesis, central to cognitive linguistics more broadly, holds that human cognition is not abstract symbol manipulation independent of the body but is fundamentally shaped by our physical form, sensorimotor capabilities, and interactions with the environment. As Johnson argues in "The Body in the Mind," meaning is rooted in bodily experience, not in correspondence to an objective external reality or in arbitrary symbolic systems.

**Image schemas** provide the crucial link between embodiment and conceptual metaphor. These pre-conceptual patterns emerge directly from bodily experience: CONTAINER, PATH, UP-DOWN, FORCE, BALANCE, CENTER-PERIPHERY. Crucially, image schemas are not visual images but abstract topological structures preserving spatial and motor logic while being schematic enough to apply across diverse situations.

Primary metaphors arise when image-schematic structures become conflated with co-occurring subjective experiences during early development. When infants experience warmth while experiencing affection, neurological structures for temperature become associated with emotion. Through repeated correlation, AFFECTION IS WARMTH becomes established. This is embodiment in a strong sense: the metaphor arises necessarily from embodied experience, not as arbitrary symbolic association.

This embodied grounding explains several key features of conceptual metaphors. It accounts for **cross-linguistic patterns**—if primary metaphors arise from universal aspects of embodied experience, we should find similar metaphors across cultures, and indeed we do. HAPPY IS UP appears in diverse languages ("feeling up," "high spirits," "lifted my mood"), plausibly grounded in the universal bodily correlation between upright posture and positive affective states. Similarly, MORE IS UP (as in "prices are rising") reflects the experiential correlation between adding quantity to a pile and increasing its height.

Embodiment also explains the **unidirectionality** of metaphorical mapping. We understand abstract concepts in terms of concrete bodily experience, not vice versa, because sensorimotor experience is more directly and richly available to cognition. We cannot understand physical warmth metaphorically in terms of affection; the mapping must proceed from the concrete to the abstract. This asymmetry is predicted by the embodiment thesis.

Empirical evidence from **cognitive neuroscience and gesture studies** supports the embodied basis of metaphorical understanding. fMRI studies show that understanding metaphors involving texture ("She had a rough day") activates sensory cortex regions for tactile processing, while action metaphors ("grasping an idea") activate motor areas for actual grasping. Similarly, speakers' spontaneous gestures reveal source domain structure—discussing timelines, they gesture horizontally; discussing argument strength, they gesture upward. These findings suggest metaphorical understanding recruits the same neural and motor systems involved in literal bodily experience, providing strong evidence that metaphorical mappings structure thought itself, not just language.

However, we must critically examine embodiment's limits. While primary metaphors may be universal due to shared embodiment, **complex metaphors show significant cultural variation**. TIME IS SPACE elaborations differ across cultures—some treat time as a landscape we move through, others as a river flowing past us; some orient future forward, others behind. This variation suggests that while embodiment provides building blocks (image schemas and primary metaphors), cultural factors and linguistic conventions shape how these assemble into complex metaphorical systems.

Furthermore, some source domains appear only loosely connected to direct bodily experience. ARGUMENT IS WAR draws not on immediate sensorimotor experience but on culturally constructed warfare. This suggests **layering** in metaphorical systems: while foundational metaphors are embodied, higher-level metaphors draw on culturally specific practices, creating multiple levels of abstraction above direct bodily experience.

Additionally, the embodiment thesis faces the **challenge of abstraction**: if all understanding is ultimately grounded in bodily experience, how do we account for highly abstract domains in mathematics, logic, or philosophy that seem to transcend bodily constraints? CMT theorists argue that even these domains are structured by metaphor (e.g., MATHEMATICAL SETS ARE CONTAINERS), but critics question whether this pushes the embodiment thesis too far, potentially making it unfalsifiable if every concept can be claimed to have some metaphorical-embodied origin.

## Conclusion

Conceptual Metaphor Theory makes several revolutionary claims: that metaphor is fundamentally conceptual rather than linguistic, that metaphors are systematic and productive, that they involve unidirectional mappings from concrete to abstract domains, and that they are ubiquitous in ordinary thought. These metaphors structure understanding through systematic correspondences that preserve inferential patterns from source to target domains. The theory relates intimately to the embodiment thesis, with primary metaphors arising directly from correlations in early embodied experience, grounded in image schemas that emerge from sensorimotor interaction with the world.

However, the relationship between CMT and embodiment is not simple reduction. While embodiment provides essential grounding for basic metaphorical mappings and explains cross-linguistic patterns, unidirectionality, and neuroscientific evidence, it does not fully determine complex metaphorical systems, which show cultural variation and involve multiple layers of abstraction above direct bodily experience. The most productive view recognizes embodiment as foundational without being totalizing—providing the experiential basis from which metaphorical thought develops without fully constraining the creative, culturally elaborated systems of meaning that humans construct. This challenges both classical rationalist views of disembodied reason and strong social constructivism, suggesting instead that cognition is shaped by universal embodiment and cultural practices. Understanding this relationship enriches our appreciation of how human cognition bridges the physical and abstract, the universal and culturally specific, through conceptual metaphor.
